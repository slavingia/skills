And if you’re not rushing, you have time to talk to customers, time to iterate, and time to test your hypothesis. [head a] Test your hypothesis A business hypothesis is just like the one you learn in fifth grade science class. It is a suggested solution for a problem that does not currently have a solution. It must be testable--able to be tested repeatedly and independently--and falsifiable--able to be proven wrong). For example: my customers will pay a fixed fee with a small premium to get their end credits quickly and efficiently produced with as many renders as they need . Every business starts by testing a hypothesis with real customers. And if you only have one customer , you can treat your startup like a whiteglove service. This may mean a phone call or sitting across the table from your customer at a local cof fee shop, helping them with their problem. The goal of these meetings is to validate this hypothesis. This takes time to test and honest reflection to recognize when you are wrong. But it is better to be wrong now , when the stakes are low , than to be wrong after you have spent five years and some of your own personal capital trying to build your idea into a business that was never meant to exist. When you are validating a hypothesis, do not ask leading questions–questions which point people to the answer you want to hear . Instead, think about creating the kind of feedback loop that author and tech entrepreneur Rob Fitzpatrick, writes about in The Mom T est . When you ask the kind of questions he recommends, the kind even your mom can’t lie to you about, you will get the honest truth because no one will know that you have a new idea for a business and that you’re testing to see if it’ s viable. For example, you shouldn’t ask: [ext] Would you pay for my product? [end ext] Instead, ask: [ext] Why haven’t you been able to fix this already? [end ext] 57
