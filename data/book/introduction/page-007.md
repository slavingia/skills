When I started Gumroad, it never occurred to me to build a business without venture capital because that was the way everyone else seemed to be doing it. Most founders will say that the bigger the company , the bigger the impact they can make on the world. If they’re being candid, they’ll mention the secrets, networks, connections, mentors, and advice that magically come along with raising millions of dollars this way . I wanted all of it: impact, access, and the prospect of making a lot of money . But I was naive. Looking back, I didn’t fully understand what I was signing up for, and once I was in, I couldn’t leave. I’d sold a chunk of my company to investors who were going to be on Gumroad’ s capitalization table--list of owners--until death do us part. While that may be the right path for some startups, it’s not the right path for many more. Yet, plenty of early stage startups still end up raising venture capital because they can’t fund their businesses in a sustainable way through profits. As a result, they’re locked into the pursuit of huge, winner-take-all markets where growth is the most important asset of their business, not revenues, profits, or sustainability . To help me reconcile those dif ferences, I had to come up with my own framework for how to start and to scale my business. The questions I had to ask myself over and over again are: What do I actually care to change? If I could fix one thing about my corner of the world, what would that be? What kind of business do I really want to build? And most importantly , what kind of impact do I really want to make? I’m not the only one. Many others have had similar realizations, including many whose stories I’ve included in this book. I call these people “minimalist entrepreneurs,” and they run what I call “minimalist businesses.” Building a minimalist business doesn’t mean settling for second best. Instead, it's about creating sustainable companies that have the flexibility to take risks to serve the greater good, all while empowering others to do the same. Being profitable, hopefully from the very beginning, means being able to focus and to stay focused on the reasons you started a business in the first place: to help others. Historically , entrepreneurs have played a crucial role in driving technological and social progress. This is even more necessary today , when big corporations are required by law to prioritize shareholder value over , among other things, actual value. In researching this book, I’ve found countless examples of businesses such as Basecamp, Wistia, Missouri Star Quilt Company , and many other honest-to-goodness, highly scalable companies incredibly focused on solving meaningful problems with beautiful products, services, and software that people love – and making a profit doing it. Each company goes about things their own way depending on the community they’re building for .
